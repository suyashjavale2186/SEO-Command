# Human Input Framework for Expert Interviews

**Scope.** This reference belongs to `expert-interview`; it governs the **conversation used to extract first-party knowledge**, not the later assembly of a content brief. The interviewer should surface decisions, trade-offs, failures, terminology, and evidence that an expert can genuinely support. [Evidence Tier: Tier 3 — Industry observation]

## Interview architecture

Open with the expert's role and boundaries. Then move from routine practice to exceptions, from claims to examples, and from outcomes to the decisions that produced them. Useful prompts include: “What do most people get wrong?”, “What happened in a real project?”, “What surprised you?”, “What failed?”, “Who should not follow this advice?”, “What evidence changed your view?”, and “What would you do differently now?” These questions are deliberately concrete because broad requests for “your expertise” tend to produce generic prose. [Evidence Tier: Tier 3 — Industry observation]

Use follow-up probes when an answer contains a conclusion without evidence. Ask what the expert observed, what artifact demonstrates it, what alternatives were considered, and where the advice stops applying. When the interviewee describes a successful result, probe for the starting condition, the decision, the constraint, and the counterfactual—not just the headline outcome. [Evidence Tier: Tier 3 — Industry observation]

## Capture format

Record the answer verbatim where permission allows, then separate **direct statement**, **interpreted insight**, **supporting artifact**, and **publication boundary**. Publication boundary matters when the expert has confidential customer details, security-sensitive information, or claims that require legal review. The final writer must never convert an unclear or incomplete answer into a fabricated first-person story. [Evidence Tier: Tier 1 — Officially documented]

## Concrete scenario

A security engineer is interviewed for a guide on phishing-resistant authentication. Instead of asking “What are the best practices?”, the interviewer asks why a recent rollout failed, what user behavior caused friction, which recovery path created risk, and what telemetry showed the problem. The engineer describes a device-enrollment bottleneck and a recovery exception. Those details become evidence-rich source material; the writer does not invent additional incidents to make the story more dramatic. [Evidence Tier: Tier 3 — Industry observation]

## Failure modes

Avoid leading questions that force the desired conclusion, yes/no prompts that suppress nuance, and “expert quotes” drafted by the interviewer rather than spoken by the expert. Stop when the expert lacks direct knowledge of the claim being tested. Preserve uncertainty when the expert says “it depends,” because that qualification may be the most valuable part of the material. [Evidence Tier: Tier 3 — Industry observation]
