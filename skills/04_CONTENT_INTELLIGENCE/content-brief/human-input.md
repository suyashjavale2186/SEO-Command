# Human Input in Content Briefs

This reference answers **what human knowledge the brief should request before drafting**. The goal is to expose proprietary experience and business context early enough that the writer can build it into the page rather than bolting it on as decoration. [Evidence Tier: Tier 3 — Industry observation]

## Brief intake sequence
Ask for: actual customer questions, common misconceptions, edge cases, implementation constraints, product limitations, examples that can be published, internal terminology, evidence owners, and situations where the recommended approach should not be used. [Evidence Tier: Tier 3 — Industry observation]

Map each answer to a section of the brief. A pricing objection might belong in the comparison criteria; an implementation failure might become a warning box in a how-to; a legal caveat may change the scope of the whole article. Human input should change the planned information architecture when it materially improves task completion. [Evidence Tier: Tier 3 — Industry observation]

## Concrete scenario
A cybersecurity company says prospects routinely ask whether a control works in a hybrid environment. The brief adds a hybrid-deployment decision branch, requests an engineer interview, and marks any performance claim as requiring verification from current product documentation. The expert's experience shapes the page, but unsupported claims are not treated as facts. [Evidence Tier: Tier 3 — Industry observation]

## Failure modes
Collecting opinions after the outline is locked; asking broad questions that yield marketing language; publishing internal anecdotes without permission; and allowing an expert's confidence to substitute for external verification are all preventable. [Evidence Tier: Tier 3 — Industry observation]

## Evidence boundary
When first-hand evidence is unavailable, do not manufacture it. Human-input prompts are an evidence-gathering mechanism, not permission to invent experience. [Evidence Tier: Tier 1 — Officially documented]
